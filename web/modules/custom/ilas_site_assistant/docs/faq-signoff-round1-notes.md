## 1. `faq_employment_fired`

One refinement: the FAQ should state explicitly that a firing that is merely "unfair," unkind, or without good cause is NOT illegal — only firings that fall within a recognized exception are actionable. This is the single most important framing point for a self-represented reader.

"termination may be wrongful if it was based on: Discrimination (race, sex, age, disability, religion, national origin)" — Accurate but incomplete. The Idaho Human Rights Act (IHRA), Idaho Code § 67-5909, prohibits discrimination on the basis of race, color, religion, sex, national origin, age, and disability. The FAQ omits color, which is a separately enumerated protected class. It should be added. Age is protected only for those 40 and older (Idaho Code § 67-5910(9)); the FAQ should say so. Two further gaps discussed below: the 5-employee threshold and the sexual orientation/gender identity/pregnancy coverage question.

"Retaliation for protected activity (reporting safety violations, filing workers' compensation claims, whistleblowing)" — Accurate in substance but misleading by omission. All three are recognized, but the legal source differs and matters a great deal:

Safety violations: protected under federal OSHA § 11(c), 29 U.S.C. § 660(c), which requires a complaint to OSHA within 30 days — an extremely short and unforgiving deadline the FAQ does not mention.
Whistleblower Protection Programs
Workers' comp: protected as a common-law public-policy tort, not by a dedicated Idaho statute. Idaho has NO workers'-comp anti-retaliation statute.
Whistleblowing: Idaho's statutory whistleblower law — the Protection of Public Employees Act, Idaho Code § 6-2101 et seq. — covers only public (government) employees.
Rippling
 Private-sector whistleblowers rely on federal law or the narrow common-law public-policy exception. The FAQ's generic "whistleblowing" wrongly implies broad state statutory protection for all workers.

"Violation of an employment contract" — Accurate but incomplete. Idaho recognizes both express and implied contract exceptions, including that an employee handbook or manual can rebut the at-will presumption and become part of the employment contract (Jenkins
ALFA International
 v. Boise Cascade Corp., 141 Idaho 233, 108 P.3d 380 (2005); Mitchell v. Zilog, Inc., 125 Idaho 709, 874 P.2d 520 (1994)). The FAQ should mention handbooks, since many users won't realize a handbook can create enforceable rights.

"Violation of public policy" — Accurate. This is the Jackson v. Minidoka exception, protecting
Racine Olson
 employees who refuse to commit unlawful acts, perform important public obligations, or exercise legal rights/privileges (Sorensen v. Comm Tek, Inc., 118 Idaho 664, 799 P.2d 70 (1990); Edmondson v. Shearer Lumber Prods., 139 Idaho 172, 75 P.3d 733 (2003)). The public policy must be rooted in Idaho statute or constitution, and whether a policy qualifies is a question of law for the court (Quiring v. Quiring, 130 Idaho 560, 944 P.2d 695 (1997)).

Missing exception — covenant of good faith and fair dealing. The FAQ omits an exception Idaho does recognize: in Metcalf v. Intermountain Gas Co., 116 Idaho 622, 778 P.2d 744 (1989), the Idaho Supreme Court
Cetient
 held for the first time that an implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing applies to all employment contracts (including at-will relationships). It is a contract claim, not a tort, and
CaseMine
 is breached only when a party nullifies or significantly impairs a benefit of the employment contract (Jones v. Micron Technology;
ALFA International
 Van v. Portneuf Medical Center, 147 Idaho 552, 212 P.3d 982 (2009)). This is narrower than many readers assume — it does not convert at-will into for-cause employment — but it belongs in a complete list.

The three procedural steps

Step 1 — "Gather documentation..." — Good advice, accurate.

Step 2 — "Request your personnel file from your employer" — This is the FAQ's clearest error/misleading statement. Idaho has no statute requiring private-sector employers to give employees or former employees access to their personnel files. The only Idaho statutory personnel-file access rights are for public employees (Idaho
Expert Employee
 Code § 74-106 and, for school employees, § 33-518). Per Holland & Hart LLP's Employers' Lawyers Blog (Aug. 2024): "Some states, including Idaho, Montana, New Mexico, Utah, and Wyoming do not have any state or regulatory provisions that apply to private-sector employment." Telling a user to "request your personnel file" as if it were an entitlement can set up false expectations and waste the limited time before deadlines run. The step should be rewritten to say the employee can ask, but a private employer is generally not legally required to comply, and that Idaho Code § 45-610 does entitle employees to certain written wage/pay-rate information on request.

Step 3 — "Note any witnesses..." — Good advice, accurate.

Filing deadlines (the most important omission)
IHRC administrative complaint: within one (1) year of the alleged discrimination (Idaho
Justia
 Code § 67-5907(1)). Filing the administrative complaint is a prerequisite to suing (Idaho
Justia
 Code § 67-5908(2)).
EEOC (federal): within 300 days in Idaho, because Idaho is a "deferral" state with a work-sharing agreement between the IHRC and EEOC. This is confirmed in Sanders v. University of Idaho College of Law (D. Idaho): the IHRC/EEOC worksharing agreement "designates the EEOC as an agent of IHRC for the purpose of receiving charges," and "because the IHRC waived Title VII's 60-day deferral period, the entire 300-day window applies." A claimant can cross-file with one agency and have it dual-filed with the other.
Stephenslawny
After the agency closes the case: a private lawsuit must be filed within 90 days of the notice of administrative dismissal/right-to-sue (Idaho
Justia
 Code § 67-5908(2)).
OSHA safety retaliation (§ 11(c)): within 30 days — a very tight deadline courts enforce strictly.
Safetyregulatory
Common-law wrongful-discharge/public-policy tort (including workers'-comp retaliation): 2 years under Idaho Code § 5-219(4), the personal-injury/tort limitation. Federal courts applying Idaho law have treated the public-policy wrongful-discharge claim as a tort governed by § 5-219(4)'s two-year period (Feltmann
govinfo
 v. Petco Animal Supplies, D. Idaho 2012). Note that a breach-of-contract or implied-covenant claim is not a tort and carries longer periods (written
Recording Law
 contract 5 years, § 5-216; oral 4 years, § 5-217).

Omitting these deadlines is the single greatest risk in the current FAQ: a user who reads it, gathers documents, and requests a (non-existent) personnel-file right could easily blow the 30-day OSHA window or the one-year IHRC window.

Recommendations

Stage 1 — Correct the outright error and add deadlines (do immediately):

Rewrite Step 2 (personnel file). Replace with: "You can request your personnel file, but be aware that Idaho law does not require private employers to provide it (only government employers must, under Idaho Code §§ 74-106 and 33-518). You may, however, request written information about your rate of pay and pay dates under Idaho Code § 45-610, and you can keep your own copies of pay stubs, reviews, and correspondence."
Add a prominent deadlines box: IHRC discrimination complaint — 1 year (Idaho Code § 67-5907); EEOC — 300 days; lawsuit after right-to-sue — 90 days; OSHA safety retaliation — 30 days; common-law wrongful discharge — 2 years. Add: "Deadlines are strict; missing one can permanently bar your claim. Act quickly and consider getting legal advice before the earliest deadline."

Stage 2 — Fix the discrimination and exception content: 3. Add color to the protected classes and note age 40+; state the IHRA's 5-employee threshold and note that federal Title VII/ADA (15+) and ADEA (20+) have higher thresholds. 4. Add a line on sexual orientation/gender identity: not covered by Idaho state law, but covered under federal Title VII (Bostock) for employers with 15+ employees; and note pregnancy is covered as sex discrimination. 5. Add the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing (Metcalf) and employee handbooks/implied contracts (Jenkins) to the exceptions list. 6. Clarify that Idaho's whistleblower statute covers only public employees, and that private-sector safety-retaliation claims go to OSHA within 30 days.

Stage 3 — Add high-value practical content for self-represented users: 7. State plainly that an "unfair" firing is not automatically illegal. 8. Add unemployment benefits: being fired does not disqualify you unless the employer proves workplace misconduct (Idaho Code § 72-1366); apply through the Idaho Department of Labor. 9. Add final paycheck timing (Idaho Code § 45-606) and the Idaho Department of Labor wage-claim route.

Benchmarks that would change these recommendations: If the IHRA is amended (e.g., "Add the Words" passes, or the legislature adds pregnancy/color clarifications), update accordingly. If Idaho enacts a private-sector personnel-file or workers'-comp anti-retaliation statute, revise Step 2 and the retaliation section. Monitor any Idaho Supreme Court decision squarely holding (rather than reciting in dicta) that workers'-comp retaliation is actionable, which would let the FAQ state it more firmly.

## 2. `faq_employment_wages`

Small claims cap is now $15,000. Your flag on the final paycheck is correct. Idaho Code § 45-606(1): upon layoff or termination by either party, all wages are due by the earlier of the next regularly scheduled payday or within 10 days, weekends and holidays excluded — and if the employee makes a written request for earlier payment, within 48 hours of receipt, weekends and holidays excluded. "By the next regular payday" alone is wrong whenever the payday falls more than 10 days out, and it hides the 48-hour written-demand right, which is exactly the lever a legal aid client should use. Recommended fix: state the earlier-of rule and add "Tip: put your request in writing — that triggers a 48-hour deadline."

## 5. `faq_mobile_home_rights`

"Reasonable and non-discriminatory": your instinct is right — the statute says neither. What it actually says: rental increases shall be uniform throughout the community, and where rents are tiered by lot/home size, amenities, or location, increases must be uniform within the same rent tier. Replace "reasonable and non-discriminatory" with "uniform across the community (or uniform within your rent tier)." Idaho has no reasonableness cap on the amount, and § 55-2006(4) expressly ties into the state ban on local rent control.

## 9. `faq_debt_dispute`

Free reports: you're right, it's outdated. Free reports from all three bureaus at AnnualCreditReport.com are now available weekly, permanently (the bureaus made the pandemic-era weekly access permanent in 2023). Change "once per year" to "free every week."
"Must remove it from your credit report": overstated. FDCPA § 809(b) (15 U.S.C. § 1692g) requires the collector to cease collection until it mails verification, and under Regulation F continued credit reporting during that window is treated as collection activity that must stop — but there is no rule that an unverified debt must be deleted from the report. Recommended language: "If the collector cannot verify the debt, it must stop collecting — including stopping credit reporting — until it does. You can separately dispute the item with the credit bureaus under the FCRA." Also add the flip side of the 30-day window: the verification right is strongest within 30 days, but you can still dispute later, and under Regulation F a collector generally may not credit-report a debt before contacting you about it.

"The original creditor never notified you": not a standalone legal ground. Cut it, or replace with a real and common one: "You never received the collector's validation notice" or "The collector can't show it owns the debt." Everything else in the answer (writing, certified mail, the other listed grounds) is sound.

## 10. `faq_wage_garnishment`

Your suspicion is confirmed: Idaho simply adopts the federal formula — the lesser of 25% of disposable earnings or the amount exceeding 30 times the federal minimum wage — restated in both § 11-207 and § 11-712, and there is no head-of-household exemption. Item 3 is flatly wrong (that's Florida law bleeding into an Idaho FAQ) and must be deleted. It's affirmatively harmful: someone might skip a real exemption claim while chasing one that doesn't exist.
Replace it with the thing Idaho does have and the FAQ omits — the deadline: under Idaho Code § 11-203 the Claim of Exemption must be delivered or mailed to the sheriff and received within 14 days after the garnishment documents are served, and the deadline is strict. The court's own Notice of Exemptions form (CAO Cv 10-10) says the same. Since the FAQ already says "you generally have a limited time," just name it: 14 days. Your second flag is also right: add that the 25% cap does not apply to child support (up to 50–65% under the federal CCPA tiers, which Idaho's statute matches verbatim, and support orders, tax debt, and bankruptcy orders bypass the ordinary cap entirely), federal student loans (15% administrative garnishment, no judgment needed), and IRS levies. Two bonus points worth one line each: only one continuing wage garnishment runs at a time, and federal law bars firing an employee over a single garnishment; and directly deposited Social Security/VA benefits get an automatic two-months-of-benefits protection at the bank (31 C.F.R. Part 212).

## 11. `faq_credit_report_errors`

Same weekly-reports fix as FAQ 9. Investigation period: 30 days is the default under FCRA § 611 (15 U.S.C. § 1681i), extendable to 45 days when the consumer submits additional relevant information during the investigation (also 45 days for disputes of items on an annual free report). Say "30 days (up to 45 in some situations)."

100-word statement: the statute (§ 1681i(b)) gives the right to file a "brief statement" of the dispute; the 100-word limit applies only when the bureau helps the consumer write it. Say "a brief statement (about 100 words)" — close enough for a FAQ, but drop the implication that 100 words is the statutory cap. Also worth adding: disputes can be sent to the furnisher as well as the bureau, and online disputes are free — the "don't pay a credit repair company" warning is excellent and should stay.

## 12. `faq_medical_debt`

Your read on the "protections" is exactly right, and the timeline confirms it: on July 11, 2025, the Eastern District of Texas vacated the CFPB's medical-debt rule in Cornerstone Credit Union League v. CFPB, agreeing it exceeded the Bureau's authority under the FCRA, and the protections consumers rely on today — paid medical collections removed, sub-$500 collections excluded, and a one-year wait before reporting — are voluntary bureau policies adopted in 2022–2023 that remain in effect as of 2026, not law. Idaho is not among the roughly 15 states that enacted their own medical-debt reporting restrictions. So "cannot appear" must become "under the credit bureaus' current voluntary policies, generally will not appear — but these are industry policies, not laws, and can change."

Charity care: confirmed — the ACA § 501(r) financial-assistance requirement applies to nonprofit (tax-exempt) hospitals. Since most Idaho hospitals are nonprofit or public district hospitals, "ask about financial assistance" remains great advice; just fix "most hospitals are required" to "nonprofit hospitals are required (and many others offer it voluntarily)." Also worth a line: Idaho's old county-indigent safety net is gone — the county medically indigent program was repealed effective March 31, 2022 along with the state CAT fund, because Medicaid expansion was expected to cover most people those programs served — so users who remember "county assistance" should be pointed to Medicaid instead.

Medicaid retroactivity: confirmed and unrestricted — Idaho's rule (IDAPA 16.03.01.150) allows coverage to begin up to three calendar months before the application month if the person was eligible during that period. Say "up to 3 months before the month you apply."

Biggest omission: the FAQ never mentions the Idaho Patient Act, the state's signature medical-debt law. Idaho Code §§ 48-301 through -312: before taking "extraordinary collection actions" (negative credit reporting, third-party collections, lawsuits, liens, garnishment), providers must submit charges to insurance within 45 days, send a consolidated summary of services within 60 days, wait 60 days after the final statement to charge interest, and wait 90 days after the final statement before any extraordinary collection action — and attorney fees recoverable against the patient are capped at $350 uncontested / $750 contested. For an ILAS audience this is arguably the single most useful section of the answer; add it as its own numbered item ("Idaho has special rules for medical debt collection…").

## 18. `faq_payday_loans`

FDCPA scope: your flag is correct. The federal FDCPA covers third-party collectors and debt buyers, not an original lender collecting its own loan. Recommended replacement: "Third-party collectors hired by (or who bought the debt from) a payday lender must follow the FDCPA. The lender itself, when collecting its own loan, is instead subject to the Idaho Credit Code, Department of Finance oversight, and the Idaho Consumer Protection Act." Note the statutory backstop that partially fills the gap: § 28-46-414(5) bars the payday lender itself from initiating collection activities while the borrower is complying with an extended payment plan.

Extended payment plan: mostly confirmed, tighten the details. Under § 28-46-414 the lender must allow the borrower, upon request, to enter an extended payment plan once during any consecutive 12-month period; it must be in writing and executed no later than the day the loan is due; it must allow at least four equal payments over at least 60 days; the obligation can't exceed the original loan; and the lender may not charge interest or additional fees as part of the plan. Two edits to the FAQ text: it's "once in any 12-month period" (not "one-time" forever), and — critically — the borrower must request it before the loan is due. Waiting until default forfeits the right; that timing point is the difference between the protection working and not.

No rate cap: confirmed — Idaho imposes no APR cap on payday loans; the maximum loan is $1,000 or 25% of gross monthly income, whichever is lower, and rollovers are limited to three. Consider adding the $1,000/25% cap and the three-renewal limit, both useful and verifiable. "You cannot be jailed" and the ACH-revocation advice are fine as written (revoke with both the lender and the bank, in writing — worth saying).

## 19. `faq_debt_statute_limitations`

Credit cards: the internal contradiction is real and, as you suspected, the underlying question is genuinely contested. Secondary sources commonly say Idaho usually treats a credit card agreement as a written contract under § 5-216, putting most card debt in the five-year window, with open accounts without a writing at four years under § 5-217 — but I found no controlling Idaho appellate holding squarely deciding whether card debt is a § 5-216 written contract or a § 5-217 open account/account stated, and collection defense practitioners argue the 4-year side. For a legal aid FAQ the safe fix is: list credit cards once, as "4 or 5 years depending on how the account is documented — if you're being sued, the shorter period may be an available defense; get advice." Don't resolve it in a FAQ; that's the piece to hold for an attorney sign-off.

Judgments: your memory is right and the FAQ is outdated. The 2015 amendment moved judgments to a 10-year regime: the legislative intent was that judgments existing before July 1, 2015 keep their 5-year life, while any renewal or new judgment on or after July 1, 2015 has a 10-year life, and today a recorded judgment lien lasts 10 years (§ 10-1110), the underlying judgment is enforceable for 11 years (§ 5-215), and a court order renewing the judgment under § 10-1111 restarts both clocks, with no cap on renewals. Replace "Judgments: 5 years (but renewable)" with "Judgments: 10 years, renewable in 10-year increments — a judgment can effectively last indefinitely if the creditor keeps renewing."

Restart rule: the warning is accurate and § 5-238 is actually broader than a signed writing: a signed written acknowledgment restarts the clock, but a bare, unsigned payment of principal or interest also works — and the Idaho Supreme Court has held payment can even revive a debt whose limitations period has already fully run. That last point deserves its own sentence in bold spirit: "Even a small payment on an old debt can restart — or revive — the clock. Get advice before paying anything on a debt you think is time-barred." One more addition: since 2021, Regulation F (12 C.F.R. § 1006.26) makes it a strict-liability violation for a collector to sue or threaten suit on a time-barred debt — but as the FAQ correctly says, some do anyway, and Idaho treats the expired deadline as an ordinary defense the debtor has to raise, so "you must raise it in your answer" should stay.
